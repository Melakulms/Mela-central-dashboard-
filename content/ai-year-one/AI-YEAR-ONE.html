MELA AI Education — Year One

DRAFT FOR HUMAN TEACHER REVIEW — 11 October 2026

52 weekly units · 4 guided hours per week · 208 hours total. Each week contains an explanation, worked example, practical task, two checkpoint questions and four session activities. Weeks 12, 28, 36, 48 and 52 are major checkpoints. This is a common foundation with 53 catalogue-linked project briefs, not 53 independently completed year-long qualifications. Existing courses have not been overwritten or marked published.

Designed for secondary-age learners, TVET, university and adult beginners. Teachers must adapt language, prerequisites, difficulty and safeguarding. Younger learners need separate age-appropriate design. No paid AI account is required: teachers may supply fictional outputs and use paper tracing. Programming can be traced on paper when a computer is unavailable. The pace is a suggested teaching plan, not a claim that reading alone takes 208 hours.

All examples and tasks are original drafts using fictional data. No lesson or question has been approved by a human teacher. All reviewer fields remain empty. English is the draft language; the five platform-language editions require translation and native-speaker review.

Each week: 60 minutes guided explanation; 60 minutes practical work; 60 minutes peer verification and revision; 60 minutes teacher assessment and correction. Carry incomplete work forward rather than recording completion automatically.

Assessment and publication gate

Teacher scores each artifact out of 20: conceptual correctness 0–4; practical execution 0–4; evidence and verification 0–4; privacy/safety/rights 0–4; independent explanation 0–4. Proposed progression threshold: 14/20 with no unresolved critical safety or factual error. Teachers must calibrate this proposal locally before adopting it. A critical safety failure blocks publication regardless of total. Retest after correction and retain both attempts.

Before publication, a verified subject teacher independently solves both checkpoint questions and completes the practical exercise. Check key correctness, acceptable variants, grade fit, source rights, cultural/language fairness and accessibility. Record reviewer identity, date, content version/hash, evidence, decision and corrections. A second reviewer checks disputed or high-stakes content. Automated checking cannot supply teacher sign-off.

Week 01 — What AI can and cannot do

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

An AI system maps inputs to outputs using rules, learned patterns or both. A model is only one part of a product: interfaces, data, people and safeguards also affect its behavior. Fluent language is not proof of understanding or truth. Before choosing AI, describe the task, the person helped and the cost of a mistake. A fixed rule or a checklist may solve a simple problem more reliably. Keep decisions affecting a person's rights or wellbeing under qualified human control.

Worked example

A school wants to remind families of opening times. A verified timetable with fixed messages is sufficient; predicting families' behavior is unnecessary.

Practical activity

List five school tasks. Choose rule-based, AI-assisted or human-only handling for each. Explain the error cost, then draw the input, processing and output for one task.

Check your understanding

Does a confident answer establish truth? Which approach suits an unchanging school timetable?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 02 — Learning goals and a baseline

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A learning goal specifies a visible capability rather than time spent using a tool. Record your initial attempt before obtaining AI help, then compare the assisted version against a rubric. Keep both versions. This reveals whether your own skill improved and whether the tool introduced errors. Use small goals you can assess without buying software. A portfolio should show what you did, the assistance received and how you checked the result. Speed matters only after accuracy and safety meet the task's requirements.

Worked example

Weak goal: learn AI. Observable goal: produce a one-page study guide with three checked claims and a correction log.

Practical activity

Write a baseline study guide from a teacher-provided paragraph. Mark uncertain statements. Define three success criteria and revise once without removing the original.

Check your understanding

Why keep a baseline? Give one measurable learning goal.

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 03 — Data, consent and minimization

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Data includes text, images, locations and records about people. Removing a name may not make a record anonymous: combinations such as school, class and a rare event can identify someone. Minimize what is collected and shared. Use invented examples in class. Consent must be meaningful and appropriate, and is not a substitute for every other obligation. Explain the purpose, who can see the data and when it will be deleted. Stop if you cannot justify a field.

Worked example

A timetable exercise needs class labels and periods, not student phone numbers, birthdays or home addresses.

Practical activity

Design a fictional attendance dataset with six rows. Remove unnecessary columns. Write a plain-language purpose and deletion rule, and identify one possible re-identification risk.

Check your understanding

Is deleting names always enough? What is minimization?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 04 — Accounts, scams and safe access

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Authentication establishes who is accessing a system; authorization decides what that person may do. A logged-in learner should not see another learner's private records. Use unique passwords and available multifactor authentication, and keep recovery information private. Inspect the destination domain before signing in. A message promising a prize in exchange for a password is not a legitimate recovery process. Report suspicious links without visiting them. Teachers must not collect learners' passwords to inspect their work.

Worked example

A link labelled MELA can point elsewhere. The visible domain and the actual destination need checking; familiar colors prove nothing.

Practical activity

On paper, classify six fictional messages as safe, suspicious or insufficient information. Design a recovery checklist that never requests a password from a teacher.

Check your understanding

How do authentication and authorization differ? Should a teacher ask for a learner's password?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 05 — Questions, context and output formats

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A useful request tells the model the task, relevant context, constraints and desired output. Only include material you are allowed to share. Ask for uncertainty to be flagged, but do not assume the model will comply. Small, explicit tasks are easier to evaluate than broad requests. A role instruction can affect style; it does not confer expertise or a professional qualification. Separate supplied source text from instructions, and state which facts may be used. Check the result against the requested format yourself.

Worked example

Request: Using only this fictional timetable, produce a three-row study plan; keep one rest period and flag conflicts.

Practical activity

Write a vague request and an improved version for the same task. Use a supplied fictional paragraph. Compare completeness, accuracy and unnecessary assumptions in two outputs.

Check your understanding

Does assigning an expert role make the model qualified? Name two useful request components.

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 06 — Verification and fabricated sources

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Treat generated claims as leads to investigate. Break a response into checkable statements. Locate the original publisher, confirm that the source exists and read the part that supports the claim. A real source may still be irrelevant or outdated. Record the date, relevant passage and your conclusion. Do not cite a source you have not inspected. If evidence is unavailable, mark the claim unverified and leave it out of a factual summary. Independent checking is especially important when a response supplies plausible-looking references.

Worked example

A response attributes a school policy to a ministry document. The document exists but discusses another school level; the claim is not supported.

Practical activity

Create a claim-evidence table for five statements in a fictional AI response. The teacher supplies a source packet with both relevant and irrelevant excerpts. Mark supported, contradicted or unknown.

Check your understanding

Can a real citation support an unrelated claim? What should happen to an unverified claim?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 07 — Bias, language and fairness

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Model performance can differ across languages, dialects and groups because data and tasks differ. One successful English example does not prove suitability for Amharic, Afaan Oromo, Tigrinya or Somali. Review meaning with fluent speakers and avoid treating dialect differences as low ability. Evaluate equivalent tasks and record errors, not personal stereotypes. Fairness involves the task, data, outcomes and the process for correction. Do not use a classroom demonstration to rank real students or make consequential decisions.

Worked example

Two equivalent prompts receive different levels of detail in two languages. This is a test result to investigate, not evidence about either language's speakers.

Practical activity

Have a qualified language reviewer compare three equivalent task pairs. Record omissions, tone and factual errors, and propose a correction procedure. Without a reviewer, label translations unreviewed.

Check your understanding

Does one translation prove language quality? Who should confirm meaning?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 08 — Intellectual honesty and creative rights

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Distinguish your contribution from assistance and borrowed material. Access to a webpage does not automatically grant permission to reproduce its content. Check the relevant license and attribution conditions, or write an original explanation. Obtain permission before using another person's voice, image or work in a project. Label AI-assisted material according to the institution's rules. Do not submit generated work as proof of a skill you cannot demonstrate. Keep a record of sources, permissions and edits for each portfolio item.

Worked example

A learner redraws a diagram and explains it in their own words, but still credits the source of the idea and checks the reuse license.

Practical activity

Prepare a source and assistance statement for a fictional presentation. Separate original content, quoted material, licensed assets and uncertain permissions. Replace any uncertain asset with original work.

Check your understanding

Does public access mean unrestricted reuse? What should an assistance statement disclose?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 09 — Safe summarization

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A summary preserves the central meaning while omitting detail. It must not invent reasons, remove important qualifications or change who made a claim. Begin by identifying the source's purpose and key points. Draft a shorter version, then compare each sentence against the original. Separate quotation from paraphrase. When material conflicts, report the conflict rather than silently combining it. Summaries of sensitive or high-stakes documents need qualified review; classroom practice should use invented or openly licensed material.

Worked example

Original: The trial improved attendance in one class; wider results are unknown. Bad summary: The program improves attendance in all schools.

Practical activity

Summarize a teacher-supplied 300-word passage in 80 words. Highlight every preserved qualification and identify one detail you deliberately omitted. Exchange versions for a source check.

Check your understanding

What went wrong in the example? Is shorter always better?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 10 — Research questions and evidence logs

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Research starts with a question narrow enough to investigate. Define the population, context and outcome without assuming the answer. Use search terms that explore competing explanations. Distinguish a primary source from commentary about it. Track author, date, method and limits, not only the title. A search result snippet is a starting point, not sufficient evidence for every claim. Keep unanswered questions visible. For school projects, a teacher should approve the scope and any data collection involving people.

Worked example

Instead of asking whether AI is good, ask how two feedback formats affect revision quality on a fictional writing exercise.

Practical activity

Turn three broad topics into focused questions. Build an evidence log from a teacher-supplied packet and write a paragraph explaining what the packet cannot establish.

Check your understanding

Why narrow a research question? Is a search snippet a complete source assessment?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 11 — Study methods and feedback

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Use AI to support retrieval practice and explanation, not to remove all thinking. Attempt a problem before looking at hints. Ask for one hint at a time, then explain the solution in your own words. Space practice over several days and return to previous mistakes. Feedback should identify the gap, give a useful next action and allow another attempt. A generated quiz may have incorrect keys, ambiguous choices or inappropriate difficulty; a teacher must check it before using it for assessment.

Worked example

A learner attempts a fraction question, receives a hint about a common denominator and then solves a new example without help.

Practical activity

Plan three short practice sessions with one-day gaps. Write two original questions, solve them independently and submit both questions and keys to a teacher for checking.

Check your understanding

Why attempt before receiving a solution? Who checks generated quiz keys?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 12 — Foundation review and safety brief

Module: Foundations and safety · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Combine the first eleven weeks into a documented use case. State the task, users, data boundaries and conditions for stopping. Include a non-AI alternative and explain why assistance is useful. List likely failures and how a human will notice them. Demonstrate the workflow using fictional data and provide an evidence log. A convincing demonstration includes a failed output and its correction. Do not claim general safety from a handful of examples; define the narrow setting actually tested.

Worked example

A study-summary helper is limited to teacher-provided text, cannot award grades and requires source-by-source checking before distribution.

Practical activity

Submit a two-page safety brief, the baseline and revised output, a five-case test table and a two-minute oral explanation without AI assistance.

Check your understanding

What belongs in a stop condition? Does a passing demonstration prove universal safety?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 13 — Documents and accessible writing

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Organize a document around its reader's task. Use descriptive headings, short paragraphs and clear labels. Separate facts from recommendations and make references traceable. Accessibility includes meaningful structure, sufficient contrast and descriptions for important images. AI can propose a structure, but a person must check whether the wording is correct and respectful. Avoid invented quotations or testimonials. On a phone, test whether the document remains readable without horizontal scrolling or tiny text. A polished layout cannot compensate for unsupported claims.

Worked example

A one-page school event notice puts the date, location and contact method before background details and describes an included map in words.

Practical activity

Create a fictional event notice in plain text first. Add headings and an accessible image description, then test it on a small screen and with a peer reading it aloud.

Check your understanding

Name two accessibility checks. Can polished layout establish accuracy?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 14 — Email and communication

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A professional message has a purpose, accurate context and a clear next step. Match tone to the relationship without pretending to be someone else. AI may draft a message, but the sender must verify names, dates, attachments and promises. Do not enter confidential correspondence into an unapproved tool. Keep teaching exercises fictional and do not send practice messages to real recipients. When a message is sensitive, a qualified person should review it before sending. An apology should not invent facts or commitments.

Worked example

A meeting request asks whether a proposed time works; it does not state that the recipient already agreed.

Practical activity

Draft a request, a clarification and a polite refusal for fictional scenarios. Underline factual claims and commitments, and rewrite any sentence that implies an unconfirmed agreement.

Check your understanding

Who remains responsible for a sent draft? What is an unconfirmed commitment?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 15 — Spreadsheets and checking calculations

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A spreadsheet separates inputs, formulas and results. Label units and preserve the original data. Check formulas using a small example you can calculate manually. Relative references move when copied; absolute references keep specified row or column positions fixed. AI suggestions can use the wrong range, unit or business rule. Treat formulas as executable logic requiring review. Avoid entering real salaries, bank records or student marks in classroom exercises. Use fictional values and retain a version before changing formulas.

Worked example

Three fictional sales of 20, 30 and 50 units total 100; an average is 100 divided by 3, not the largest value.

Practical activity

Build a six-row fictional shop table with quantity and price. Calculate line totals and a grand total. Hand-check two rows and deliberately insert an error for a partner to locate.

Check your understanding

Why label units? How can a formula be independently checked?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 16 — Presentations and truthful charts

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A presentation should help an audience understand an argument, not merely display text. Each slide needs a clear point supported by evidence. Select charts that fit the data and label axes, units and sources. Cropped axes or omitted categories can mislead. Use original or licensed visuals and avoid decorative images that imply evidence you do not have. Rehearse the explanation without reading every word. Provide a text alternative for important visual content, especially when sharing on phones.

Worked example

A bar chart compares four fictional class project counts with a zero baseline and clearly labelled categories, rather than using oversized decorative icons.

Practical activity

Create five slides for a fictional school project. Include one chart, a source note and a limitations slide. Ask a peer to explain the chart without your narration.

Check your understanding

What can an omitted category hide? Why include limitations?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 17 — Images and visual evaluation

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Generated images may contain inaccurate objects, text or relationships. Use them as creative drafts rather than scientific evidence. Define the intended message, composition and accessibility needs before generating. Check every visible label and remove misleading details. Obtain permission before using someone's likeness, and do not impersonate real people. For exact charts or diagrams, use data-driven or vector tools with verified labels. Keep a record of generation and edits so viewers can understand how the image was made.

Worked example

A generated poster contains a misspelled date. The designer corrects it and checks all text before sharing; the image is not treated as a source.

Practical activity

Design an original fictional study-club poster. Produce a sketch or draft, list five visual checks and write an image description. No image-generation account is required.

Check your understanding

Should a generated image be used as proof of an event? What requires checking?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 18 — Audio, video and consent

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Audio and video combine information with powerful cues about identity. A synthetic voice or edited clip can mislead even when the words are accurate. Use your own voice or properly authorized material, and label synthetic media clearly. Plan a short script, storyboard and captions before recording. Check that captions preserve meaning and that music does not obscure speech. Never clone a real person's voice without appropriate permission. Classroom projects should avoid identifiable children and private locations unless the school's safeguarding process authorizes them.

Worked example

A fictional explainer uses a student's own narration and captions, with a title card stating that the illustrations are AI-assisted.

Practical activity

Make a 60-second storyboard with six frames, a narration script and captions. A paper storyboard is acceptable. Include consent and source notes for every planned asset.

Check your understanding

Why do synthetic voices need clear disclosure? What is the offline alternative?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 19 — Customer service and escalation

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A service assistant should answer within an approved knowledge base and escalate uncertainty. It must not invent refund rules, disclose another customer's information or promise actions it cannot perform. Separate information retrieval from changes to accounts. For complaints, acknowledge the issue, ask only necessary questions and explain the next step. Keep a record of the source used. Use fictional customers during testing, and include cases where the correct response is to refer the matter to a human.

Worked example

A fictional return policy allows returns within seven days. A request on day ten is escalated rather than met with an invented exception.

Practical activity

Write a ten-question FAQ from a supplied fictional policy. Add three out-of-scope cases and a handoff script that avoids collecting unnecessary personal details.

Check your understanding

Can an assistant invent a refund exception? What should it do when policy is unclear?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 20 — Career preparation without fabrication

Module: Productivity and communication · 240 guided minutes · Draft

AI can help structure a CV, identify skill gaps and practice interview answers. It must not invent qualifications, employment, references or achievements. Match each claim to evidence you actually possess. Use the official vacancy description and check eligibility before investing time. An interview answer should distinguish personal contribution from team work. Do not pay someone merely because they promise a job. Practice in fictional scenarios and avoid sharing identity documents with unverified recruiters or tools.

Worked example

A learner replaces 'expert developer' with a truthful description of a small school coding project and explains what they personally built.

Practical activity

Create a fictional CV and an evidence table linking every claim to a sample portfolio item. Record an interview answer and identify one skill needing further practice.

Check your understanding

Is tailoring a CV permission to invent experience? Where should eligibility be checked?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 21 — Data tables and measurement

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A row usually represents one observation and a column one variable. Define what each variable measures, its unit and the allowed values before collecting data. An identifier is not necessarily a meaningful numerical quantity. Missing data is different from zero, and repeated rows may be duplicates rather than additional observations. Keep a data dictionary so another person can interpret the table. Measurement choices determine which questions can be answered; a convenient dataset may omit what actually matters.

Worked example

In a fictional rainfall table, a blank cell means no measurement was recorded, while zero means a measured dry day.

Practical activity

Create a ten-row fictional rainfall table and a data dictionary. Include one missing value and one duplicate, and document how each will be handled without inventing measurements.

Check your understanding

Are missing and zero equivalent? What does a data dictionary record?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 22 — Data cleaning and provenance

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Cleaning improves consistency but can also remove meaningful information. Preserve raw data, record each transformation and explain why it is justified. Normalize dates and units carefully, detect duplicates and flag impossible values. Do not silently replace missing values with convenient guesses. Some errors need a source correction rather than an automatic rule. Provenance describes where data came from and how it changed. A reproducible cleaning log allows another person to understand and repeat the work.

Worked example

Values recorded in metres and centimetres cannot be averaged directly. Convert to one unit and retain the original value and conversion rule.

Practical activity

Clean a teacher-supplied fictional table containing inconsistent dates, mixed units and duplicates. Submit the raw table, cleaned table and transformation log with unresolved issues flagged.

Check your understanding

Why preserve raw data? Should every unusual value be deleted?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 23 — Descriptive statistics

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A mean is the sum divided by the number of values; a median is the middle after sorting. Outliers can strongly affect the mean, so report the distribution and context. A percentage requires a clear numerator and denominator. A small sample does not establish what happens in a whole population. Statistics summarize observations; they do not automatically explain causes. Use fictional data and show intermediate calculations so a teacher can check your reasoning.

Worked example

For 2, 3, 4, 5 and 26, the mean is 8 and the median is 4. The large final value changes the mean substantially.

Practical activity

Calculate mean, median, minimum and maximum for two invented datasets with the same median but different extremes. Explain which summary is most informative and why.

Check your understanding

What is the median in the example? Does a mean prove a cause?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 24 — Visualization and uncertainty

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Choose a chart according to the question: categories, change over time or the relationship between quantities. Label units and explain missing observations. A trend drawn through sparse points may imply certainty that is not justified. Show the data you actually have rather than inventing smooth intermediate measurements. Distinguish observed values from forecasts. When presenting a small sample, describe its limits in plain language. A reader should be able to reconstruct the main comparison from the labels and numbers.

Worked example

Four monthly counts show a rise, but they do not prove the same rise will continue next year. A forecast must be labelled separately.

Practical activity

Draw a bar chart and a line chart from fictional data. Add source, units and a one-sentence limitation. Ask a peer to identify which quantities are observed and which are hypothetical.

Check your understanding

Why separate forecasts from observations? Which chart suits category comparisons?

Week 25 — Python values and expressions

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A program follows explicit instructions; it does not understand your intention automatically. Python represents values using types such as numbers, strings and booleans. Quotation marks distinguish text from variable names. Read code line by line and predict the result before running it. Errors are useful signals about syntax or assumptions. Use a teacher-approved environment and never paste unknown commands from a model into a terminal. Paper tracing is sufficient when a computer is unavailable.

Worked example

With price = 20 and quantity = 3, total = price * quantity produces 60. The string '20' is text and should not be confused with the number 20.

Practical activity

Trace five short calculations on paper, then write a fictional purchase-total program. Test zero quantity and a changed price. Explain each variable and unit.

Check your understanding

What is the total in the example? Why distinguish a string from a number?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 26 — Conditions, loops and functions

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Conditions choose a branch, loops repeat work and functions package a reusable task. Define inputs and outputs before writing a function. Test boundaries such as zero, an empty list and a value exactly at a threshold. Repetition should terminate for a known reason. A function with a clear purpose is easier to inspect than a long script with unrelated actions. Do not execute model-generated code until you understand its file, network and credential access.

Worked example

A fictional shipping rule charges 10 when quantity is below 5 and zero otherwise. Test quantities 4, 5 and 6 to check the boundary.

Practical activity

Write or trace a function for the shipping rule and a loop calculating charges for six orders. Add three boundary tests and explain the expected results before execution.

Check your understanding

Which quantity is the boundary? Why test an empty list?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 27 — Files, structured data and safe parsing

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Structured formats such as CSV and JSON store data according to conventions. Check field names, types, sizes and allowed values before using imported content. Data from a file is not an instruction to execute. Keep secrets out of examples and repositories, and use a separate copy for practice. A malformed row should produce a clear validation message rather than silently changing meaning. Limit file size and preserve an error report so the original source can be corrected.

Worked example

A JSON record with quantity: 'many' fails a numeric validation rule. The importer reports the record instead of treating the value as zero.

Practical activity

Design a JSON schema on paper for a fictional book catalogue. Create three valid and three invalid records, with an expected validation message for each invalid record.

Check your understanding

Should imported text be executed as code? What should happen to invalid quantity data?

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 28 — Data project checkpoint

Module: Data and programming · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A small data project should be understandable from raw input to conclusion. State a question, define fields, clean the data, calculate summaries and present limitations. Preserve the original file and a transformation log. Separate observations from recommendations. Test calculations against small manual examples and allow a peer to reproduce your work. Do not use classroom models to judge real people. This checkpoint evaluates reasoning and reproducibility rather than the sophistication of the software used.

Worked example

A fictional library-loan report counts books by category and notes that missing return dates prevent reliable estimates of loan duration.

Practical activity

Submit a ten-row fictional dataset, data dictionary, cleaning log, two charts and a one-page conclusion. Give a peer your instructions and record any reproduction failures.

Check your understanding

Week 29 — Supervised learning and labels

Module: Machine learning · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Supervised learning uses examples with target labels to learn a relationship between inputs and outputs. Features are the input measurements and labels are the outcomes being predicted. The task must be defined before selecting a model. Poor labels can teach a system the wrong pattern, even when training appears successful. A prediction is an estimate, not a fact about a person. Use low-risk fictional examples and compare with a simple baseline before adding complexity.

Worked example

For fictional parcels, weight and distance are features and delivery cost is the numeric label. Customer names are not needed for this task.

Practical activity

Create twelve fictional parcel records. Identify features, label and unnecessary fields. Propose a simple fixed-cost baseline and explain what would count as an improvement.

Check your understanding

Which field is the label? Does a complex model automatically beat a baseline?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 30 — Training, validation and test sets

Module: Machine learning · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Training data fits the model, validation data supports choices and test data estimates performance after those choices are fixed. Keep related observations together when separating sets so near-duplicates do not leak across them. Future information must not be used to predict the past. Repeatedly changing a model after viewing test results makes the test less independent. Document the split and retain untouched examples for final evaluation. A tiny classroom split demonstrates the process but cannot establish production reliability.

Worked example

Two versions of the same fictional parcel record belong in the same split; otherwise the test may reward memorization.

Practical activity

Divide a fictional dataset into three sets on paper. Identify duplicate and time-related leakage risks. Write rules for when the test set may be opened.

Check your understanding

Why protect the test set? Where should duplicate versions go?

Evidence to submit

Completed task, source/assistance log, a failure or correction, and an independent oral explanation.

Week 31 — Regression and error

Module: Machine learning · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Regression predicts a numerical value. Evaluate predictions by comparing them with observed targets on suitable held-out data. Absolute error measures distance without cancellation; averaging signed errors can hide large mistakes of opposite directions. Always report the unit and compare against a simple baseline. Large errors may matter more in some applications, so choose a metric consistent with the real task. Do not infer causal effects from a predictive relationship alone.

Worked example

Targets 10 and 20 with predictions 8 and 24 have absolute errors 2 and 4, giving a mean absolute error of 3.

Practical activity

Calculate errors for six fictional delivery-cost predictions and a fixed-price baseline. Identify the worst case and explain whether the model's improvement is practically useful.

Check your understanding

What is the example's mean absolute error? Do opposite errors cancel under absolute error?

Evidence to submit

Week 32 — Classification and confusion matrices

Module: Machine learning · 240 guided minutes · Draft

Classification predicts a category. A confusion matrix separates correct and incorrect decisions by actual and predicted class. Accuracy alone can hide failure on a rare class. Precision asks how many predicted positives are correct; recall asks how many actual positives were found. Define the positive class and denominator before calculating either. These metrics describe different error tradeoffs, and no single metric fits every task. Classroom exercises should use harmless fictional categories rather than sensitive judgments about people.

Worked example

Of 10 predicted damaged parcels, 8 are actually damaged; precision is 8/10. If 16 parcels were damaged in total, recall is 8/16.

Practical activity

Check your understanding

What are the example's precision and recall? Is accuracy always sufficient?

Week 33 — Overfitting and baselines

Module: Machine learning · 240 guided minutes · Draft

A model overfits when it learns details of training examples that do not generalize well. High training performance with substantially worse held-out performance is a warning. More complexity is not always useful. Compare with a simple baseline, improve data quality and evaluate changes on a validation set. Record both successful and failed experiments. Do not keep selecting the model that happens to score best on a reused test set. Choose the simplest approach that meets the justified requirements.

Worked example

A rule memorizing every training parcel gets zero training error but cannot price a new route. A simpler distance-based estimate may generalize better.

Week 34 — Clustering and pattern discovery
